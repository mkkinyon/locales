Yes! On reflection, I have several additional observations. Two are fairly immediate consequences of the theorem, but a third suggests a more general structural investigation. I think they're worth recording separately from the referee report.

## **1. A complete description of the fitted sublocales**

This is perhaps the cleanest consequence that the packet doesn't explicitly state.

Since the open and closed sheets are complementary in the sublocale coframe, we have an isomorphism

\\[ \mathcal S(Z)\cong\mathcal S(K)\times\mathcal S(K), \\]

given by

\\[ G\longmapsto(G\wedge F,G\wedge U). \\]

In these coordinates, (B8) becomes the remarkably simple formula

\\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Sat}\_{\mathcal A}(H,C) =(H,C\vee(P\wedge H)). } \tag{1} \\]

Consequently:

**Corollary.** A sublocale \\(G=H_F\vee C_U\\) is fitted in \\(Z\\) if and only if

\\[ \boxed{P\wedge H\leq C.} \tag{2} \\]

In particular, a sublocale \\(H_F\\) of the closed sheet is fitted in \\(Z\\) precisely when

\\[ P\wedge H=0. \\]

This gives a complete description of the fitted sublocales of these two-sheet locales.

It also explains the Cantor example very economically: every point sublocale is disjoint from \\(P\\), but the entire first sheet is not.

I would promote (2) to an explicit corollary.

## **2. An exact criterion for arbitrary-join preservation**

Formula (1) suggests something stronger than the Cantor counterexample.

For a family \\((H_i,C_i)\\), we have

\\[ \operatorname{Sat}\left(\bigvee_i(H_i,C_i)\right) = \left( \bigvee_i H_i,\\, \bigvee_i C_i\vee \left(P\wedge\bigvee_i H_i\right) \right), \\]

whereas

\\[ \bigvee_i\operatorname{Sat}(H_i,C_i) = \left( \bigvee_i H_i,\\, \bigvee_i C_i\vee \bigvee_i(P\wedge H_i) \right). \\]

Taking every \\(C_i=0\\) establishes the converse implication in the following characterization.

**Corollary.** Saturation preserves arbitrary joins if and only if

\\[ \boxed{ P\wedge\bigvee_i H_i = \bigvee_i(P\wedge H_i) } \tag{3} \\]

for every family of sublocales \\(H_i\leq K\\).

Thus the exact obstruction is an order-theoretic property of \\(P\\) inside \\(\mathcal S(K)\\).

For example, (3) holds whenever \\(P\\) is complemented in the sublocale lattice. This follows from the distributivity properties of complemented elements in coframes.&#x20;

This puts the Cantor example into a larger framework. Its significance is not merely that arbitrary-join preservation fails, but that it produces a nonzero \\(P\\) for which (3) fails maximally on the family of point sublocales.

I would consider investigating which fitted sublocales \\(P\\) satisfy (3). This seems like a natural little problem in its own right.

## **3. Can every fitted sublocale occur as the interface?**

This is the observation I find most interesting.

The theorem suggests that the saturation behavior depends only on \\(P\\), rather than on the full gluing frame \\(L\\).

Indeed, suppose we start with *any fitted sublocale* \\(P\hookrightarrow K\\), where \\(K\\) is zero-dimensional. Write \\(i:P\hookrightarrow K\\) and define

\\[ \boxed{ L_P= \\{(c,v)\in\mathcal O(K)^2: i^\*(c)\leq i^\*(v)\\}. } \tag{4} \\]

I claim that this construction realizes the prescribed interface.

First, \\(L_P\\) is a subframe: the defining inequality is preserved under coordinatewise arbitrary joins and finite meets because \\(i^\*\\) is a frame homomorphism.

Second, \\(L_P\\) contains every diagonal pair \\((c,c)\\), as well as \\((0,1)\\).

Third,

\\[ (1,v)\in L_P \iff i^\*(v)=1 \iff P\leq O_K(v). \\]

Consequently, the interface computed from (B1) is

\\[ \bigwedge\_{P\leq O_K(v)}O_K(v) =\operatorname{fit}\_K(P) =P. \\]

So we obtain:

**Proposed realization theorem.** Every fitted sublocale \\(P\\) of a zero-dimensional locale \\(K\\) can occur as the exact cross-sheet specialization interface of a two-sheet gluing satisfying the packet's hypotheses.

There's more. If an arbitrary admissible gluing \\(L\\) has interface \\(P\\), the argument used in (B3) shows that

\\[ (c,v)\in L\implies i^\*(c)\leq i^\*(v). \\]

Therefore

\\[ \boxed{L\subseteq L_P.} \\]

In other words, \\(L_P\\) is the **largest admissible gluing frame with prescribed interface&#x20;**\\(P\\).

This is a potentially useful classification result. All such gluings with the same \\(P\\) have identical saturation formulas under the canonical identification of their sublocale lattices.

Notice the distinction: their full frames of opens can differ, but their canonical specialization and fitting behavior is controlled by the same \\(P\\).

I would want to examine this more carefully before declaring it consolidated, but the proof looks quite direct.

## **4. A characterization of the&#x20;**\\(T_1\\)**&#x20;phenomenon**

There is also a neat interpretation of the original motivation.

Assume \\(K\\) is spatial and zero-dimensional. Then the gluing \\(Z\\) is spatial, with its points arranged in two copies of the points of \\(K\\).

The cross-sheet specialization relation on ordinary points is exactly

\\[ p_F\preceq p_U \iff p\text{ is a point of }P. \\]

All same-sheet specialization relations are equality, and there is no reverse cross-sheet specialization.

It follows that

\\[ \boxed{ Z\text{ is }T_1 \iff P\text{ has no points}. } \tag{5} \\]

Therefore the phenomenon we have been studying—nontrivial localic specialization in a spatial sober \\(T_1\\) locale—occurs precisely when

\\[ P\neq0 \quad\text{but}\quad \operatorname{pt}(P)=\varnothing. \\]

This brings us directly back to the original point-free motivation. The cross-sheet interface is literally invisible to the ordinary specialization graph.

## **My assessment**

I see two possible ways to use these observations.

The conservative option would be to add (1)–(3) as corollaries to the existing consolidation. They are straightforward consequences of the audited theorem.

The more interesting option would be to investigate the realization construction (4). It raises some natural questions:

- How much of the gluing frame \\(L\\) is determined by \\(P\\)?
- What are the smallest admissible gluing frames with a given \\(P\\)?
- Which additional properties—compactness, coherence, local compactness, sobriety—can be imposed while prescribing \\(P\\)?
- Can we use the construction to produce particularly simple examples of nonzero pointless specialization interfaces?

The last question appeals to me. The Cantor construction is ingenious, but (4) suggests that we might be able to construct such examples much more systematically.

**My strongest recommendation is to record the elementary corollaries and examine the realization theorem.** These observations are additional deductions, not parts of the completed blind audit, so I would give them a fresh mathematical check before relying on them.

I don't think this necessarily warrants a major new research direction, but it might substantially clarify what the open/closed-interface theorem is really telling us.
